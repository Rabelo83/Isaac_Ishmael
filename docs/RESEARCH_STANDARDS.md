# Research and citation standards

## Questions before conclusions

Frame each record as a bounded question about a particular place, period, population, right, or proposal. Separate what happened, what an actor claimed, what a legal body decided, and what remains unresolved. Do not transfer a legal standard from one forum or period without explaining its relevance.

## Source selection

Use original legal texts, judgments, treaties, archival records, inscriptions, excavation reports, and critical textual editions where relevant. Use peer-reviewed research and reputable academic publishers for contextual evaluation. Museums, archives, and university collections can provide provenance and access. Institutional reputation is helpful but does not by itself establish a particular claim.

Government, movement, and mediator publications are primary evidence of their stated positions; their assertions about opponents require independent evaluation. Journalism may establish contemporary reporting and supply leads but should not replace an accessible original document. Search snippets, AI output, unsourced graphics, and Wikipedia are not final evidentiary authorities.

For a contested material factual claim, seek independent corroboration and serious counterevidence. Multiple publications repeating the same account do not count as independent corroboration. A primary legal text can establish its own wording without a second source. If only one evidentiary source survives, explain that limitation rather than manufacture corroboration.

## Citations and reader access

Every published externally verifiable assertion needs a citation attached to the relevant sentence or record. Each citation must include:

- Stable source ID, full title, author or issuing body, and document type.
- Document or publication date, edition/version, and original language.
- Clickable direct URL, DOI, or authoritative catalog record; prefer the original issuer.
- Page, paragraph, article, verse, archival reference, or another precise locator where available.
- Access date and access status: open, paywalled, catalog only, unavailable, or archived.
- Relationship to the assertion: supports, qualifies, contradicts, or contextualizes.
- An explanation of what the source establishes and its material limits.

Where useful, provide an official landing page and a direct document link. Add lawful open versions or clearly labeled archive links when originals are unavailable. A working link does not prove that the source supports the claim; inspect the cited passage. Never imply a paywalled or inaccessible full text was read if only its metadata was available.

Keep original-language references. Link official English and Spanish versions where available. Label project translations and preserve the original text for key terminology. Use short necessary quotations with attribution; do not reproduce whole copyrighted works or maps without permission or a suitable license.

## Evidence labels

Use descriptive categories rather than numerical confidence scores:

- Textual or religious tradition.
- Contemporary record or material evidence.
- Historical reconstruction.
- Attributed party position.
- Legal finding or legal instrument.
- Project synthesis.

Separately record review status: lead, source inspected, draft, reviewed, or published. A reviewed record may still be disputed. Record whether disagreement concerns authenticity, dating, translation, facts, interpretation, or legal applicability. Give an explanation for uncertainty; do not label all positions equally supported merely for symmetry.

## Legal authority and comparison

For every legal record, capture the forum, jurisdiction, parties, question presented, instrument type, procedural stage, relevant date, applicable law, operative result, reasoning, remedies, scope of binding effect, separate opinions, and subsequent treatment. Distinguish party submissions from court findings, interim measures from merits decisions, and judgments from advisory opinions and political resolutions.

The ICJ Statute distinguishes sources used by the Court in Article 38 and limits the binding force of a decision to the parties and particular case in Article 59. Therefore, this project must justify the use of other cases rather than assume automatic binding precedent. See the [official Statute, Articles 38 and 59](https://legal.un.org/avl/pdf/ha/sicj/icj_statute_e.pdf). This is a methodological starting point, not a complete account of international law.

Assess directly relevant authorities before analogies. Select comparative cases by legal issue and factual structure, not by whether the outcome favors a desired position. Document similarities, differences, temporal limits, jurisdictional limits, competing readings, and what the case cannot establish. Include contrary authorities. Do not import a criminal burden of proof into territorial or historical research.

## Maps and population figures

Every map requires a date or interval, geographic extent, source, legend, provenance, reuse permission, and explanation of uncertainty. Distinguish administrative boundaries, claimed borders, armistice lines, military control, settlement, and private ownership. A political-control map cannot stand in for a land-ownership map. Offer accessible textual equivalents.

Population figures require year, geographic denominator, categories, method, and uncertainty. Do not compare differently bounded areas or differently defined communities without explaining the mismatch. Do not silently project present-day identities or borders backward.

## Review and correction

Review the exact supporting passages, counterevidence, translation, dates, and scope before publication. Check both languages for equivalent meaning and equivalent certainty. Record last substantive review separately from last link check. Keep a dated correction history and stable record IDs. Time-sensitive developments need fresh primary-source review at release; the planning date is not a verified news cutoff.
